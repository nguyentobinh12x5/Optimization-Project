\section{Problem Modeling}\label{sec:modeling}

\subsection{Decision Variable and Constants}

The \textbf{decision variable} is the weight vector $w \in \R^{N}$ with $N = 98$, where $w_i$
is the fraction of total capital allocated to stock $i$. A negative $w_i$ denotes a short
position.

The \textbf{constants} are the statistical inputs, estimated from historical data before the
optimization runs and held fixed while it does: $\muhat \in \R^{N}$, the sample mean of daily
returns; $\Sig \in \mathbb{S}^{N}_{+}$, their sample covariance; and $\Ssqrt$, the symmetric
positive-semidefinite square root of $\Sig$, so that $\Ssqrt\Ssqrt = \Sig$. The three scalars $\kappa, \gamma, \lambda \ge 0$ are \textbf{hyperparameters, not decision
variables}. The optimizer treats them as fixed constants; they are chosen by the modeller when
exploring the solution family (\cref{sec:insample}) or by validation on held-out data
(\cref{sec:backtest}).

All quantities are in \textbf{daily} return units. No annualization occurs inside the
optimizer; annualized figures appear only in the performance table of \cref{sec:backtest}.

\subsection{Objective Function}

The optimization problem is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main}
\min_{w \in \R^{N}} \quad
  \underbrace{-\muhat^\top w}_{\text{expected return}}
  \;+\; \underbrace{\kappa \norm{\Ssqrt w}_2}_{\text{robustness}}
  \;+\; \underbrace{\gamma\, w^\top \Sig w}_{\text{variance}}
  \;+\; \underbrace{\lambda \norm{w}_1}_{\text{sparsity}}
\qquad \text{s.t.} \quad \one^\top w = 1 .
\end{equation}

\centering

the four competing objectives identified in \cref{sec:identification}.}
\label{tab:terms}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Term} & \textbf{Role} \\
\midrule
$-\muhat^\top w$
  & Minimizing the negative expected return is maximizing expected return. \\[2pt]
$\kappa \norm{\Ssqrt w}_2$
  & Worst-case adjustment for uncertainty in $\muhat$; $\kappa$ sets how cautious. \\
  & Derived rather than assumed in \cref{sec:correctness}. \\[2pt]
$\gamma\, w^\top \Sig w$
  & Portfolio variance; $\gamma$ is the risk-aversion coefficient. \\[2pt]
$\lambda \norm{w}_1$
  & Convex surrogate for the number of holdings; also caps gross leverage. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\subsection{Constraints}

The single explicit constraint is the \textbf{budget constraint} $\one^\top w = 1$: the
portfolio is fully invested. \textbf{There is no constraint $w \ge 0$} --- short selling is
permitted, a deliberate decision made at the outset rather than an omission, and one to keep in
mind when reading \cref{sec:insample}, since a reader who assumes a long-only portfolio will
misinterpret every weight reported there. \Cref{sec:backtest} does add $w \ge 0$ for the deployed
strategy, and explains there why the out-of-sample study is long-only while the in-sample study
is not.

\subsection{Type of Optimization Problem}

Problem \cref{eq:main} is \textbf{convex, nondifferentiable, and second-order-cone
representable}. The convexity argument is given in \cref{sec:correctness}; the conic
representability is established by exhibiting the epigraph reformulation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:socp}
\begin{aligned}
\min_{w,\,t,\,s,\,u} \quad & -\muhat^\top w + \kappa t + \gamma s + \lambda \one^\top u \\
\text{s.t.} \quad
  & \norm{\Ssqrt w}_2 \le t, \\
  & \norm{\Ssqrt w}_2^2 \le s, \\
  & -u_i \le w_i \le u_i, \quad i = 1,\dots,N, \\
  & \one^\top w = 1 .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The first constraint is a second-order cone constraint on $(\Ssqrt w, t)$; the second is a
rotated second-order cone, using $w^\top \Sig w = \norm{\Ssqrt w}_2^2$; the third is the standard
linear splitting of the $\ell_1$ norm, tight at the optimum because $\lambda \ge 0$ pushes each
$u_i$ down to $|w_i|$; the fourth is affine. Every constraint is thus affine or conic and the
objective linear in $(w,t,s,u)$, so \cref{eq:main} is a second-order cone program --- which is
what makes it legitimate to hand the same problem to an interior-point conic solver in
\cref{sec:insample} and treat its answer as independent ground truth.

Nondifferentiability shapes the algorithm. Two of the four terms are nonsmooth:
$\kappa\norm{\Ssqrt w}_2$ fails to be differentiable wherever $\Ssqrt w = 0$, and
$\lambda\norm{w}_1$ at every point where some $w_i$ is zero --- which, for a penalty whose
purpose is to drive coordinates to zero, is exactly where the solution lives. Plain gradient
descent therefore does not apply, and \cref{sec:method} builds a proximal-subgradient method.

\subsection{Modeling Assumptions}

The model rests on the following assumptions, stated as assumptions rather than facts:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Daily returns are drawn from a distribution whose first two moments are stable across
        the estimation window, so that a sample mean and sample covariance are meaningful
        inputs to a forward-looking decision.
  \item The historical estimates $\muhat$ and $\Sig$ are the relevant inputs; the model
        contains no separate forecasting layer.
  \item Capital is perfectly divisible, so fractional share quantities are permitted.
  \item There are no position limits, sector limits, or liquidity limits beyond the budget
        constraint.
  \item Transaction costs are \emph{absent} from \cref{eq:main}. They are reintroduced only in
        the backtest of \cref{sec:backtest}, at $0.20\%$ of turnover per rebalance.
  \item Short positions, where they occur, are assumed executable without borrowing cost, and
        portfolio weights are assumed re-set at each evaluation point rather than allowed to
        drift --- except in \cref{sec:backtest}, where both drift between rebalances and the
        cost of correcting it are modelled explicitly.
\end{enumerate}
