\section{In-Sample Results and Cross-Verification}\label{sec:insample}

\subsection{Estimating the inputs}

$\muhat$ is the column-wise sample mean of the return panel and $\Sig$ its sample covariance
(with $\text{ddof} = 1$), symmetrized to remove floating-point asymmetry. The square root
$\Ssqrt$ is built by \textbf{eigendecomposition rather than Cholesky factorization}, and the
reason is not stylistic: a sample covariance estimated with $N = 98$ from $T = 965$
observations is positive \emph{semi}definite at best, and in floating point carries a few
eigenvalues that are marginally negative. Cholesky requires strict positive definiteness and
simply fails. The implementation instead computes $\Sig = V\operatorname{diag}(\ell)V^\top$,
clips negative $\ell_i$ to zero, and sets
$\Ssqrt = V\operatorname{diag}(\sqrt{\ell})V^\top$. The reconstruction is accurate to
$\norm{\Ssqrt\Ssqrt - \Sig}_F / \norm{\Sig}_F = 1.831 \times 10^{-15}$, i.e.\ to machine
precision.

\subsection{Solver behaviour and the effect of $\lambda$}

\Cref{tab:demo} reports two representative solves. In both cases $\one^\top w = 1$ holds to
$10^{-12}$, and the reported zeros are \textbf{exact} zeros produced by the proximal step, not
small values falling below a display threshold. That distinction is the practical payoff of
\cref{prop:jointprox}: the model's sparsity is real, so the count of nonzero weights is a
count of positions the investor would actually open.

\Cref{fig:sparsity} traces the number of active positions as $\lambda$ sweeps a logarithmic
grid. The count falls as $\lambda$ rises, but \textbf{not strictly monotonically}, and the
reason is structural rather than numerical: $\lambda$ enters only through the threshold
$t = \alpha_k\lambda$, and raising $t$ also shifts the multiplier $\nu^\star$ that enforces the
budget constraint in \cref{prop:jointprox}. A larger $\lambda$ can therefore move
$z_i + \nu^\star$ far enough for a coordinate to re-enter the active set even as most others
leave it. The trend is unambiguous; the monotonicity is not, and the figure is reported as it
is rather than smoothed.

\subsection{The effect of the robust term}

Raising $\kappa$ from $0$ to $1$, with $\gamma$ and $\lambda$ held fixed, \textbf{increases}
both the number of active positions and the Herfindahl--Hirschman concentration index
(\cref{fig:robust}). The portfolio becomes \emph{more} concentrated. The folk intuition ---
robust means cautious means diversified --- predicts the opposite.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{fig5_robust_effect.png}
\caption{Effect of the robust term. Turning $\kappa$ on increases concentration on this
universe, contrary to the usual intuition.}
\label{fig:robust}
\end{figure}

A mechanism is available, and it is the degree-one homogeneity established in
\cref{eq:robust-derivation}. Because $\kappa\norm{\Ssqrt w}_2$ is a norm rather than a squared
norm, its subgradient $\kappa\,\Sig w / \norm{\Ssqrt w}_2$ has magnitude that does \emph{not}
shrink as $\norm{w}$ shrinks --- unlike the variance term's gradient $2\gamma\Sig w$, which
weakens proportionally. The robust term thus exerts a scale-independent pull in a fixed
direction. Given the eigenstructure of a covariance matrix estimated from 98 heavily co-moving
Vietnamese large caps, where a single market factor dominates, that pull is served more
efficiently by concentrating weight along a low-variance combination of a few names than by
spreading it across many correlated ones.

This is an empirical observation on one dataset accompanied by a plausible mechanism. It is
\emph{not} a theorem about every $\Sig$, and it should not be generalized into one.

\subsection{Cross-verification against an interior-point solver}

The argument for this subsection is worth stating before its numbers. The method of
\cref{sec:method} is first-order, hand-written, and specific to this problem. CLARABEL
\citep{clarabel2024}, invoked through CVXPY \citep{diamond2016}, is a general-purpose
second-order interior-point solver operating on the conic reformulation \cref{eq:socp}. The two
share no code, no algorithmic family, and no failure modes. \Cref{sec:correctness} established
that \cref{eq:main} is convex, so its optimal value is unique and two correct solvers must
agree on it.

Agreement on the objective is the weaker test. Agreement on the \textbf{active set} is far
stronger, because the support is a discrete object: a solution that is numerically close but
structurally wrong --- for instance one produced by the prox-then-project scheme of
\cref{sec:proxbug} --- will generally get the support wrong even while landing near the right
objective value. That is exactly how the bug was caught.

The result is unambiguous: the \textbf{maximum relative objective gap is $0.000775\%$}, and the
\textbf{active sets match exactly on all six of six parameter sets}, with every run of the
hand-written solver reporting convergence. \Cref{fig:proxcvx} shows the individual weights
lying on the line $y = x$.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{fig6_prox_vs_cvxpy.png}
\caption{Weights from the hand-written solver against those from CVXPY/CLARABEL, for a
parameter set with $\lambda > 0$.}
\label{fig:proxcvx}
\end{figure}

The last row of \cref{tab:cvxpy} is a deliberate control. With $\lambda = 0$ the $\ell_1$
penalty is switched off entirely and both solvers return a fully dense portfolio, 98 positions
active. Without that row, identical supports could in principle reflect nothing more than two
methods trivially zeroing the same coordinates; the dense case shows the agreement survives
when there is no sparsity to agree about.

One implementation detail deserves disclosure. CVXPY rejects a covariance matrix that is not
strictly positive semidefinite in floating point, and the estimated $\Sig$ has a few marginally
negative eigenvalues, so the code wraps it in \texttt{cp.psd\_wrap} to suppress that check.
This relaxes a numerical tolerance --- the same concession made by clipping eigenvalues when
forming $\Ssqrt$ --- and does not alter the data passed to either solver.
