\section{In-Sample Results}\label{sec:insample}

\subsection{Estimating the inputs}

$\muhat$ is the column-wise sample mean of the return panel and $\Sig$ its sample covariance
($\text{ddof} = 1$), symmetrized. The square root $\Ssqrt$ is built by \textbf{eigendecomposition
rather than Cholesky factorization}: a sample covariance estimated with $N = 98$ from $T = 965$
observations is positive \emph{semi}definite at best and in floating point carries a few
marginally negative eigenvalues, on which Cholesky --- requiring strict positive definiteness ---
simply fails. The implementation computes $\Sig = V\operatorname{diag}(\ell)V^\top$, clips
negative $\ell_i$ to zero, and sets $\Ssqrt = V\operatorname{diag}(\sqrt{\ell})V^\top$,
reconstructing $\Sig$ to $\norm{\Ssqrt\Ssqrt - \Sig}_F / \norm{\Sig}_F = 1.831 \times 10^{-15}$.
The same near-boundary issue recurs when $\Sig$ is handed to CVXPY: it rejects a covariance
matrix that is not strictly positive semidefinite in floating point, so the code wraps $\Sig$ in
\texttt{cp.psd\_wrap} to suppress that check. This relaxes the same numerical tolerance as
clipping negative eigenvalues above, and does not alter the data passed to the solver.

\subsection{Solving the problem, and the effect of $\lambda$}

Problem \cref{eq:main} is solved directly with CVXPY \citep{diamond2016}, calling CLARABEL
\citep{clarabel2024} on the conic reformulation \cref{eq:socp}. \Cref{tab:demo} reports two
representative solves on the full return panel. In both, $\one^\top w = 1$ holds to $10^{-12}$.
``Active'' means $|w_i| > 10^{-4}$: an interior-point method does not produce exact zeros the way
a proximal step would, so coordinates it drives toward zero settle in the $10^{-9}$--$10^{-6}$
range rather than landing on it exactly (the largest such residual across both solves below is
$8.7\times10^{-6}$, four orders of magnitude below the active threshold) --- so the active count
is a judgement call at a fixed, generous threshold, not an exact combinatorial statement.

\Cref{fig:sparsity} traces active positions as $\lambda$ sweeps a logarithmic grid. The count
falls as $\lambda$ rises but \textbf{not strictly monotonically}, for a structural rather than
numerical reason: raising the $\ell_1$ weight shifts where the budget constraint $\one^\top w=1$
binds, so a larger $\lambda$ can move a coordinate's marginal trade-off far enough for it to
re-enter the active set even as others leave. The trend is unambiguous; the monotonicity is not,
and the figure is reported as it is rather than smoothed.

\subsection{The effect of \texorpdfstring{$\gamma$}{gamma}}\label{sec:gammasensitivity}

$\gamma$ is the risk-aversion coefficient of \cref{eq:main}: \cref{fig:gammasens} sweeps it over
$[0.1, 100]$ with $\kappa=\lambda=0$ held fixed (the general, short-selling-permitted model, so
that the effect of $\gamma$ alone is isolated from the constraints A/B add later), and reports
the deployed portfolio's expected return $\muhat^\top w^\star$ and volatility
$\sqrt{w^{\star\top}\Sig w^\star}$ at each value.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{fig_gamma_sensitivity.png}
\caption{Portfolio expected return and volatility as $\gamma$ sweeps $[0.1,100]$ on a log grid,
$\kappa=\lambda=0$ fixed. Both fall by roughly three orders of magnitude across the sweep.}
\label{fig:gammasens}
\end{figure}

Both curves fall monotonically and smoothly --- unlike the $\lambda$ sweep above, there is no
structural non-monotonicity here, because $\gamma$ enters \cref{eq:main} through a strictly
convex quadratic rather than a piecewise-linear penalty whose active set can reorganize. At the
low end ($\gamma=0.1$), with no long-only constraint and no leverage cap, the optimizer is
willing to take a $137\%$-volatility, $37\%$-expected-daily-return position --- an extreme,
heavily-levered bet that is only sane in-sample against a fixed $\muhat$, and is the same
mechanism (small effective risk-aversion, unbounded short-selling) that \cref{sec:ef} later shows
produces catastrophic losses out of sample. At the high end ($\gamma=100$), both curves flatten
toward the minimum-variance corner of the mean--variance frontier: return and volatility are both
small and further increases in $\gamma$ buy little additional risk reduction. The two long-only
variants tested in \cref{sec:backtest} search $\gamma\in\{1,5,10\}$, a range chosen to sit in the
steep, sensitive part of this curve rather than either extreme --- consistent with the finding
there that $\gamma$'s selected value swings between adjacent months rather than settling
(\cref{sec:results7}): in this steep region, a small shift in the estimated $\muhat,\Sig$ from one
month to the next can move the validation-optimal $\gamma$ substantially.

\subsection{The effect of the robust term}

Raising $\kappa$ from $0$ to $1$ with $\gamma, \lambda$ fixed \textbf{increases} both the number
of active positions and the Herfindahl--Hirschman concentration index: the portfolio becomes
\emph{more} concentrated, where the folk intuition --- robust means cautious means diversified
--- predicts the opposite. The mechanism is the degree-one homogeneity of
\cref{eq:robust-derivation}: because $\kappa\norm{\Ssqrt w}_2$ is a norm rather than a squared
norm, its subgradient $\kappa\,\Sig w / \norm{\Ssqrt w}_2$ does \emph{not} shrink in magnitude as
$\norm{w}$ shrinks, unlike the variance gradient $2\gamma\Sig w$, so it exerts a scale-independent
pull in a fixed direction --- which, given a covariance matrix dominated by a single market
factor, is served more efficiently by concentrating weight along a low-variance combination of a
few names than by spreading it across many correlated ones. This is an empirical observation on
one dataset with a plausible mechanism, \emph{not} a theorem about every $\Sig$.
\Cref{app:robust} gives the figure.
