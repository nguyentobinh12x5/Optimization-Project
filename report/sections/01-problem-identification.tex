\section{Problem Identification}\label{sec:identification}

An investor has one unit of capital and must decide how to divide it among the stocks of the
VN100 basket, the index of the hundred largest and most actively traded companies listed in
Vietnam. Ninety-eight of those tickers survive the data cleaning described in
\cref{sec:data}, and the decision is informed by four years of daily closing prices. The
question is simply: what fraction of the capital should go into each stock?

The textbook answer is Markowitz mean--variance analysis \citep{markowitz1952}: estimate each
stock's average return and the covariance between stocks, then trade expected return off
against portfolio variance. In practice that answer is incomplete for three reasons, and each
one motivates a term in the model built in \cref{sec:modeling}.

\paragraph{The inputs are estimates, and the mean is a bad one.}
An average return computed from a few years of daily data is a noisy quantity. An optimizer
handed such an estimate will happily concentrate capital in whichever stocks happened to drift
upward during the sample, because it has no way to distinguish a genuine tendency from an
accident of the period. The resulting portfolio is optimal for the past and arbitrary for the
future. This is not a hypothetical concern: it is the standard explanation for why optimized
portfolios so often fail to beat a naive equal-weight benchmark out of sample
\citep{demiguel2009}. A useful model should therefore ask not only ``what is best if the
estimate is right?'' but ``what is still acceptable if the estimate is somewhat wrong?''

\paragraph{Holding ninety-eight stocks is expensive.}
A mathematically optimal portfolio typically assigns a nonzero weight to every available
asset, including weights of a fraction of a percent. Each of those positions has to be bought,
rebalanced, monitored, and eventually sold, and each transaction costs money. An investor
generally prefers a portfolio of ten or twenty names to one of ninety-eight, even at a small
cost in theoretical optimality, and that preference is an objective worth encoding rather than
an inconvenience to be ignored.

\paragraph{Risk still matters.}
Variance of the realized portfolio return remains a real concern, and the classical quadratic
penalty remains the natural way to express it.

\medskip

The goal of this project is therefore to find portfolio weights that balance four competing
objectives at once --- expected return, variance, sensitivity to a mis-estimated mean, and the
number of positions actually held --- and to do so with an optimization algorithm written from
scratch rather than delegated to an off-the-shelf solver, so that the optimization machinery is
the object of study rather than a black box.

The remainder of the report follows the structure of the problem.
\Cref{sec:modeling} states the model formally and classifies it.
\Cref{sec:correctness} argues that the model faithfully represents the informal problem above.
\Cref{sec:method} derives and presents the solution algorithm.
\Cref{sec:data} describes the data.
\Cref{sec:insample} reports in-sample results and verifies the hand-written solver against an
independent one.
\Cref{sec:backtest} tests the strategy out of sample against an equal-weight benchmark, and
\cref{sec:conclusion} discusses efficiency, solution quality, and limitations.
