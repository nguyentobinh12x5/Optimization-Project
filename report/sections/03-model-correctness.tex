\section{Model Correctness}\label{sec:correctness}

This section argues that \cref{eq:main} faithfully represents the informal problem of
\cref{sec:identification}, term by term, and establishes the convexity property that the
verification strategy of \cref{sec:insample} depends on.

\subsection{The return term}

If $r$ denotes the vector of realized returns, the portfolio return is $w^\top r$ and, by
linearity of expectation, $\mathbb{E}[w^\top r] = w^\top \mathbb{E}[r] \approx \muhat^\top w$.
Minimizing $-\muhat^\top w$ is therefore exactly maximizing the estimated expected return of
the portfolio. Nothing is approximated in the translation from the informal objective to the
formal one except the estimate $\muhat$ itself --- which is the subject of the next
subsection.

\subsection{The robust term is derived, not asserted}

Calling a penalty ``robust'' is not an argument. Here the term is derived, and the derivation
is what justifies its particular form.

Suppose the true mean return vector is not known exactly, but is known to lie in an ellipsoid
centred on the estimate and shaped by the covariance,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uncertainty-set}
\mathcal{U} = \left\{\, \mu = \muhat + \Ssqrt u \;:\; \norm{u}_2 \le \kappa \,\right\}.
\end{equation}
A cautious investor evaluates a portfolio not by its return under the point estimate but by its
return under the least favourable member of $\mathcal{U}$. That worst case is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:robust-derivation}
\max_{\mu \in \mathcal{U}} \; \bigl(-\mu^\top w\bigr)
 \;=\; -\muhat^\top w + \max_{\norm{u}_2 \le \kappa} \bigl(-u^\top \Ssqrt w\bigr)
 \;=\; -\muhat^\top w + \kappa \norm{\Ssqrt w}_2 ,
\end{equation}
where the final equality is the definition of the dual norm of the Euclidean norm, with the
maximum attained at $u^\star = -\kappa\,\Ssqrt w / \norm{\Ssqrt w}_2$.

Three consequences follow, and each matters later.

First, the second and third terms of \cref{eq:main} taken together are not a heuristic
penalty bolted onto a mean--variance objective: the pair
$-\muhat^\top w + \kappa\norm{\Ssqrt w}_2$ \emph{is} the exact worst-case expected return over
the uncertainty set \cref{eq:uncertainty-set}. This places the model in the standard robust
portfolio framework \citep{goldfarb2003} and, more generally, in the robust-counterpart
machinery of convex optimization \citep{boyd2004}.

Second, the ellipsoid is shaped by $\Ssqrt$ rather than being spherical. Directions in which
the historical estimate is statistically less reliable --- those with higher variance --- are
given proportionally more room to move against the portfolio. That is the appropriate shape
for an uncertainty set describing sampling error in a mean, not an arbitrary choice.

Third, and with consequences that reappear in \cref{sec:insample}, the term
$\kappa\norm{\Ssqrt w}_2$ is \textbf{positively homogeneous of degree one} in $w$: it is a
norm, not a squared norm, so scaling $w$ by $c > 0$ scales the term by $c$ rather than $c^2$.
This structural fact --- forced on the model by the derivation, not chosen for convenience ---
turns out to explain an empirical result in \cref{sec:insample} that would otherwise look
simply wrong.

\subsection{The variance term}

The quantity $w^\top \Sig w$ is the variance of the portfolio return under the same
second-moment estimate, so $\gamma$ is the classical risk-aversion coefficient and
\cref{eq:main} reduces exactly to Markowitz mean--variance optimization
\citep{markowitz1952} when $\kappa = \lambda = 0$. That limiting case is a useful sanity check
on any implementation, since the solution then has a closed form, and it is exercised
directly by the project's test suite.

\subsection{The sparsity term does two jobs}

What the investor described in \cref{sec:identification} actually wants is a limit on the
\emph{number} of holdings, that is, a constraint on $\norm{w}_0$. That quantity is nonconvex
and discontinuous, and imposing it would destroy every guarantee the rest of the model relies
on --- including the global optimality that makes the verification in \cref{sec:insample}
meaningful. The term $\lambda\norm{w}_1$ is its standard convex surrogate, the same
substitution that underlies the Lasso \citep{tibshirani1996} and its established use in
portfolio construction \citep{brodie2009}.

There is a second effect specific to this problem. Under the budget constraint,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:leverage}
\norm{w}_1 \;\ge\; \bigl|\one^\top w\bigr| \;=\; 1 ,
\end{equation}
with equality precisely when every $w_i \ge 0$. The quantity $\norm{w}_1$ therefore measures
\textbf{gross leverage} --- total long exposure plus total short exposure --- and its excess
over $1$ is exactly twice the short exposure. Because \cref{eq:main} permits short selling,
this second role is economically meaningful rather than incidental: the same term that
encourages few holdings also discourages financing large long positions with large short ones.
The model obtains a leverage control for free from a term introduced for an entirely different
purpose.

\subsection{Convexity, and why it licenses the cross-check}

Each term of \cref{eq:main} is convex in $w$: $-\muhat^\top w$ is affine;
$\norm{\Ssqrt w}_2$ is a norm composed with a linear map; $w^\top \Sig w$ is a quadratic form
with $\Sig \succeq 0$; and $\norm{w}_1$ is a norm. A sum of convex functions with nonnegative
coefficients is convex, and the feasible set $\{w : \one^\top w = 1\}$ is affine and hence
convex. Problem \cref{eq:main} is therefore convex, so \textbf{every local minimum is a global
minimum} and the optimal value is unique.

This is not a decorative observation. It is what makes the verification strategy of
\cref{sec:insample} a correctness test rather than a comparison of opinions: since the optimal
value is unique, two solvers that are both correct \emph{must} agree on it, and a disagreement
is proof that at least one of them is wrong. Without convexity, two solvers reaching different
answers would be unremarkable.

\medskip

Finally, it is worth being explicit about what the model does not capture. It contains no true
cardinality constraint, only its convex relaxation; no sector, position, or liquidity limits;
no market-impact model; and no mechanism for the estimates $\muhat$ and $\Sig$ to be anything
other than backward-looking sample statistics. These limitations are revisited in
\cref{sec:conclusion}.
