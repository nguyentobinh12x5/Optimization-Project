\section{Model Correctness}\label{sec:correctness}

\subsection{The return and variance terms}

By linearity of expectation the portfolio return satisfies
$\mathbb{E}[w^\top r] = w^\top\mathbb{E}[r] \approx \muhat^\top w$, so minimizing
$-\muhat^\top w$ is exactly maximizing estimated expected return; nothing is approximated in
that translation except the estimate $\muhat$ itself. Likewise $w^\top\Sig w$ is the variance
of the portfolio return under the same second-moment estimate, so $\gamma$ is the classical
risk-aversion coefficient and \cref{eq:main} reduces exactly to Markowitz mean--variance
optimization \citep{markowitz1952} when $\kappa = \lambda = 0$. That limiting case has a closed
form and is exercised directly by the project's test suite as a sanity check.

\subsection{The robust term is derived, not asserted}

Calling a penalty ``robust'' is not an argument. Here the term is derived, and the derivation
justifies its particular form. Suppose the true mean is not known exactly but lies in an
ellipsoid centred on the estimate and shaped by the covariance,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uncertainty-set}
\mathcal{U} = \left\{\, \mu = \muhat + \Ssqrt u \;:\; \norm{u}_2 \le \kappa \,\right\}.
\end{equation}
A cautious investor evaluates a portfolio by its return under the least favourable member of
$\mathcal{U}$, which is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:robust-derivation}
\max_{\mu \in \mathcal{U}} \; \bigl(-\mu^\top w\bigr)
 \;=\; -\muhat^\top w + \max_{\norm{u}_2 \le \kappa} \bigl(-u^\top \Ssqrt w\bigr)
 \;=\; -\muhat^\top w + \kappa \norm{\Ssqrt w}_2 ,
\end{equation}
the final equality being the definition of the dual norm of the Euclidean norm, attained at
$u^\star = -\kappa\,\Ssqrt w / \norm{\Ssqrt w}_2$.

Three consequences follow. First, the first two terms of \cref{eq:main} are not a heuristic
penalty bolted onto a mean--variance objective: together they \emph{are} the exact worst-case
expected return over \cref{eq:uncertainty-set}, placing the model in the standard robust
portfolio framework \citep{goldfarb2003, boyd2004}. Second, the ellipsoid is shaped by $\Ssqrt$
rather than being spherical, so directions in which the estimate is statistically less reliable
are given proportionally more room to move against the portfolio --- the appropriate shape for
sampling error in a mean, not an arbitrary one. Third, the term is \textbf{positively
homogeneous of degree one} in $w$: a norm, not a squared norm, so scaling $w$ by $c > 0$ scales
it by $c$ rather than $c^2$. This structural fact, forced on the model by the derivation rather
than chosen, explains an empirical result in \cref{sec:insample} that would otherwise look
simply wrong.

\subsection{The sparsity term does two jobs}

What the investor of \cref{sec:identification} wants is a limit on the \emph{number} of
holdings, a constraint on $\norm{w}_0$. That quantity is nonconvex and discontinuous, and
imposing it would destroy the global optimality that makes the verification of
\cref{sec:insample} meaningful. The term $\lambda\norm{w}_1$ is its standard convex surrogate,
the substitution underlying the Lasso \citep{tibshirani1996} and its established use in
portfolio construction \citep{brodie2009}.

A second effect is specific to this problem. Under the budget constraint,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:leverage}
\norm{w}_1 \;\ge\; \bigl|\one^\top w\bigr| \;=\; 1 ,
\end{equation}
with equality precisely when every $w_i \ge 0$. So $\norm{w}_1$ measures \textbf{gross
leverage} --- total long plus total short exposure --- and its excess over $1$ is exactly twice
the short exposure. Because \cref{eq:main} permits short selling, this role is economically
meaningful rather than incidental: the term that encourages few holdings also discourages
financing large long positions with large short ones. The model obtains leverage control for
free from a term introduced for a different purpose.

\subsection{Convexity, and why it licenses the cross-check}

Each term of \cref{eq:main} is convex: $-\muhat^\top w$ is affine; $\norm{\Ssqrt w}_2$ is a
norm composed with a linear map; $w^\top\Sig w$ is a quadratic form with $\Sig \succeq 0$; and
$\norm{w}_1$ is a norm. A nonnegative combination of convex functions is convex, and
$\{w : \one^\top w = 1\}$ is affine and hence convex. Problem \cref{eq:main} is therefore
convex, so \textbf{every local minimum is global} and the optimal value is unique.

This is not decorative. It is what makes the verification of \cref{sec:insample} a correctness
test rather than a comparison of opinions: since the optimal value is unique, two correct
solvers \emph{must} agree on it, and disagreement proves at least one is wrong. Without
convexity, two solvers reaching different answers would be unremarkable.

Finally, what the model does not capture: no true cardinality constraint, only its relaxation;
no sector, position, or liquidity limits; no market-impact model; and no mechanism for $\muhat$
and $\Sig$ to be anything but backward-looking sample statistics. These are revisited in
\cref{sec:conclusion}.
