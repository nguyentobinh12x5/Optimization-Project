\section{Model Correctness}\label{sec:correctness}

\subsection{The return and variance terms}

By linearity of expectation, $\mathbb{E}[w^\top r] = w^\top\mathbb{E}[r] \approx \muhat^\top w$,
so minimizing $-\muhat^\top w$ is exactly maximizing estimated expected return; nothing is
approximated beyond the estimate $\muhat$ itself. Likewise $w^\top\Sig w$ is the variance of the
portfolio return under the same second-moment estimate, so $\gamma$ is the classical
risk-aversion coefficient and \cref{eq:main} reduces exactly to Markowitz mean--variance
optimization \citep{markowitz1952} when $\kappa = \lambda = 0$ --- a closed-form limiting case
exercised directly by the test suite as a sanity check.

\subsection{The robust term is derived, not asserted}

Calling a penalty ``robust'' is not an argument; here the term is derived. Suppose the true mean
lies in an ellipsoid centred on the estimate and shaped by the covariance,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uncertainty-set}
\mathcal{U} = \left\{\, \mu = \muhat + \Ssqrt u \;:\; \norm{u}_2 \le \kappa \,\right\}.
\end{equation}
A cautious investor evaluates a portfolio by its return under the least favourable member of
$\mathcal{U}$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:robust-derivation}
\max_{\mu \in \mathcal{U}} \; \bigl(-\mu^\top w\bigr)
 \;=\; -\muhat^\top w + \max_{\norm{u}_2 \le \kappa} \bigl(-u^\top \Ssqrt w\bigr)
 \;=\; -\muhat^\top w + \kappa \norm{\Ssqrt w}_2 ,
\end{equation}
the final equality being the dual norm of the Euclidean norm, attained at
$u^\star = -\kappa\,\Ssqrt w / \norm{\Ssqrt w}_2$.

Three consequences follow. The first two terms of \cref{eq:main} are thus not a heuristic penalty
but the \emph{exact} worst-case expected return over \cref{eq:uncertainty-set}, placing the model
in the standard robust portfolio framework \citep{goldfarb2003, boyd2004}. The ellipsoid is
shaped by $\Ssqrt$ rather than spherical, so directions where the mean estimate is statistically
less reliable get proportionally more room to move against the portfolio. And the term is
\textbf{positively homogeneous of degree one} --- a norm, not a squared norm, so scaling $w$ by
$c>0$ scales it by $c$ --- a structural fact forced by the derivation that explains an empirical
result in \cref{sec:insample} that would otherwise look simply wrong.

\subsection{The sparsity term does two jobs}

What the investor of \cref{sec:identification} wants is a limit on the \emph{number} of holdings,
a constraint on $\norm{w}_0$ --- nonconvex and discontinuous, and imposing it would destroy the
global optimality that makes the verification of \cref{sec:insample} meaningful. The term
$\lambda\norm{w}_1$ is its standard convex surrogate, the substitution underlying the Lasso
\citep{tibshirani1996} and its established use in portfolio construction \citep{brodie2009}.

A second effect is specific to this problem. Under the budget constraint,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:leverage}
\norm{w}_1 \;\ge\; \bigl|\one^\top w\bigr| \;=\; 1 ,
\end{equation}
with equality precisely when every $w_i \ge 0$, so $\norm{w}_1$ measures \textbf{gross
leverage} --- long plus short exposure --- its excess over $1$ being exactly twice the short
exposure. Because \cref{eq:main} permits short selling, this role is economically meaningful: the
term that encourages few holdings also discourages financing large longs with large shorts, a
leverage control obtained for free from a term introduced for a different purpose.

\subsection{Convexity, and what it does and does not guarantee}

Each term of \cref{eq:main} is convex: $-\muhat^\top w$ affine, $\norm{\Ssqrt w}_2$ a norm
composed with a linear map, $w^\top\Sig w$ a quadratic form with $\Sig \succeq 0$, and
$\norm{w}_1$ a norm. A nonnegative combination of convex functions is convex, and
$\{w : \one^\top w = 1\}$ is affine, so \cref{eq:main} is convex: \textbf{every local minimum is
global} and the optimal value is unique.

This is not decorative: a proximal-subgradient iteration (\cref{sec:algorithm}) is a local
procedure, and convexity is what promotes whatever it converges to into the \emph{global}
optimum --- the same property that lets an independent interior-point solve serve as a check on
it (\cref{sec:verification}), since two correct solvers on a convex problem must agree. What
convexity does \textbf{not} guarantee is that a given \emph{implementation} is bug-free: a
mis-derived subgradient or a prematurely terminated root-find still returns a feasible point
satisfying $\one^\top w = 1$, silently. That is why \cref{sec:verification} checks the code, not
just the math.

Finally, what the model does not capture: no true cardinality constraint, only its relaxation; no
sector, position, or liquidity limits; no market-impact model; and no mechanism for $\muhat$ and
$\Sig$ to be anything but backward-looking sample statistics. These are revisited in
\cref{sec:conclusion}.
