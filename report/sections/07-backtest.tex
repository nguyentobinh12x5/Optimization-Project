\section{Walk-Forward Out-of-Sample Backtest}\label{sec:backtest}

\subsection{Why the in-sample results are not enough}

\Cref{sec:insample} estimates $\muhat$ and $\Sig$ from the full return panel and then optimizes
against those same estimates. The resulting $w^\star$ is optimal for data it has already seen.
A portfolio can look excellent under that procedure purely because $\muhat$ absorbed the
particular noise of that particular period --- and nothing in the in-sample objective can
distinguish that case from genuine predictive content.

Separating the two requires an evaluation in which \textbf{every decision at time $t$ uses only
data dated strictly before $t$}. That is what this section does.

\subsection{Design}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Rolling window.} Each rebalance uses the 24 months of history immediately
        preceding it, split into 18 months for estimation ($E$) and the following 6 months for
        validation ($V$).
  \item \textbf{Schedule.} Monthly rebalancing on the first trading day of each month.
  \item \textbf{Long-only.} The deployed problem adds $w \ge 0$, unlike \cref{eq:main}, since
        the target investor has no short access. As established in \cref{sec:method}, the
        simplex constraint makes $\norm{w}_1 \equiv 1$, so $\lambda$ cannot affect the solution
        and is dropped from the search rather than tuned pointlessly.
  \item \textbf{Hyperparameter selection, per period.} The full grid
        $(\kappa,\gamma) \in \{0, 0.5, 1, 2\} \times \{1, 5, 10\}$ --- twelve combinations ---
        is fitted on $E$ and scored by Sharpe ratio ($r_f = 0$) on $V$, which was not used for
        fitting. The best combination is then refitted on $E \cup V$ to produce the weights
        actually deployed for the coming month.
  \item \textbf{Shrinkage.} Ledoit--Wolf shrinkage \citep{ledoit2004} is applied throughout,
        for both selection and the deployed fit.
  \item \textbf{Costs.} $0.20\%$ of turnover at each rebalance. The first period carries
        turnover $1$, since the portfolio is bought from cash.
  \item \textbf{Benchmark.} Equal-weight $1/N$, rebalanced on the identical schedule under the
        identical fee formula, so the comparison is like-for-like rather than a
        fully-costed strategy against a frictionless index.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Interpretation}

\paragraph{The win is real.}
The strategy beats equal-weight $1/N$ on both cumulative return and Sharpe ratio. This is not a
foregone conclusion: $1/N$ is a notoriously difficult baseline, and the finding that optimized
portfolios routinely fail to beat it out of sample is well established
\citep{demiguel2009}. It is worth stating that a loss would have been reported here just as
readily --- the backtest was built before its outcome was known, and a negative result on a
hard baseline would have been an equally legitimate finding.

\paragraph{The win is narrow, and it is paid for.}
Volatility is roughly $44\%$ higher than the benchmark's ($28.68\%$ against $19.88\%$) and the
maximum drawdown is about $1.8$ times deeper ($-37.89\%$ against $-20.91\%$). The Sharpe ratio
already nets this out, so the risk-adjusted edge ($0.5244$ against $0.4523$) is genuine --- but
it is a modest edge, and an investor experiences the drawdown directly rather than
experiencing the Sharpe ratio.

\paragraph{Costs are the binding practical problem.}
Total fees are about seven times the benchmark's ($3.44\%$ against $0.49\%$) over the same 25
rebalances, driven by an average monthly turnover near $69\%$. The cause is diagnosable rather
than mysterious. Because $(\kappa,\gamma)$ is re-selected every period against a six-month
validation window --- short, and therefore noisy --- $\gamma$ swings across
$\{1, 5, 10\}$ between consecutive months without ever settling, and each swing rewrites the
target portfolio wholesale. A secondary observation from the same selection record is that
$\kappa$ is chosen as $0$ in most periods: a short-horizon Sharpe criterion rarely rewards the
robust term on this data, which is consistent with robustness being a protection against tail
scenarios that a two-year sample may simply not contain. Set against a raw cumulative return
advantage of roughly nine percentage points, three points of fees --- before any slippage or
market impact --- consumes a material share of the gain.
