\section{Walk-Forward Out-of-Sample Backtest}\label{sec:backtest}

\subsection{Why the in-sample results are not enough}

\Cref{sec:insample} estimates $\muhat$ and $\Sig$ from the full return panel and then optimizes
against those same estimates, so $w^\star$ is optimal for data it has already seen. A portfolio
can look excellent purely because $\muhat$ absorbed the noise of that period, and nothing in the
in-sample objective distinguishes that from genuine predictive content. Separating them requires
an evaluation in which \textbf{every decision at time $t$ uses only data dated strictly before
$t$}.

\subsection{Design}

Each rebalance --- monthly, first trading day, $494$ out-of-sample days, $25$ rebalances ---
uses a rolling $24$-month window split into $18$ months estimation ($E$) and $6$ months
validation ($V$), sliding forward one month at a time (\cref{tab:timeline}; \cref{app:timeline}
works a full turnover and cost example). Within each period: fit every hyperparameter candidate
on $E$; score each by Sharpe realized on $V$, data never seen during fitting; keep the winner;
re-estimate $\muhat,\Sig$ on the full $E\cup V$ and refit at the winning hyperparameters --- those
weights are deployed for the single next month. Consecutive periods share $23$ of $24$ months of
data, yet \cref{sec:interpretation} still finds hyperparameters swinging between adjacent
periods: a six-month validation slice is a noisy yardstick even when the data barely changes.

Every variant is fitted on $E$ and scored by Sharpe ($r_f=0$) on $V$ uniformly, so the comparison
is not an artifact of differing selection criteria. Shrinkage is Ledoit--Wolf \citep{ledoit2004}
throughout; costs are $0.20\%$ of turnover per rebalance, turnover summing \emph{absolute} weight
change against the drifted (not previous target) portfolio (\cref{app:timeline}); first-period
turnover is $1$. \textbf{The walk-forward runs on CLARABEL rather than the proximal-subgradient
method of \cref{sec:algorithm}}: this section re-solves the problem $12$--$60$ times per rebalance
across $25$ rebalances, and the $\sim6\times$ per-solve cost measured in \cref{sec:cost} would
turn a sub-minute run into several minutes. The substitution is licensed, not assumed:
\cref{tab:verification} shows the two solvers agree to six significant figures with identical
active sets, so the choice is one of speed and cannot move the reported performance; where the
distinction matters, in the exact-sparsity counts of \cref{sec:insample}, the proximal method is
the one used.

\textbf{A warning about Sharpe for E and F.} E and F lose $100\%$ of capital yet report
\emph{positive} Sharpe ($0.55$, $0.42$), E's exceeding benchmark C's ($0.45$) --- arithmetic, not
a misprint. The Sharpe ratio averages daily returns \emph{arithmetically}, while CumRet
\emph{compounds} them; the two diverge by the volatility drag $\overline{r}-\tfrac12\sigma^2$. At
E's $245.5\%$ annualized volatility the drag $\approx\tfrac12(2.455)^2\approx301\%$ exceeds the
arithmetic mean $\approx135\%$, so a comfortably positive average still compounds to zero. The
lesson: \textbf{Sharpe is the wrong yardstick for a portfolio with unbounded leverage}, and that
bears directly on \cref{sec:ef}, since validation Sharpe is exactly the criterion selecting
$(\kappa,\gamma,\lambda)$ each rebalance --- a criterion blind to volatility drag cannot see the
leverage that destroys these two variants.

\subsection{A concentration problem, and a constraint to fix it}\label{sec:cap}

A and B already beat both benchmarks. But the \emph{number of names held} swings wildly month to
month --- B's active count ranges $1$--$14$, mean $7.56$ (full trace in \cref{app:backtest}), and
in February 2025 the entire portfolio concentrated in a single ticker on the strength of its
trailing sample mean and lost $9.47\%$ the following month when that edge did not persist.
Nothing in \cref{eq:main} stops this: a long-only QP's KKT conditions place the optimum at a
vertex or low-dimensional face of the simplex, with no floor on how many names that face touches.

\textbf{Adding a constraint an investor would actually want.} Rather than hard-code a cap, we
parameterize it by $K$, the minimum number of names the investor wants held --- a choice the
investor makes, not the model --- and add to \cref{eq:main}, for A and B only,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:capk}
w_i \le \frac{1}{K}, \qquad i = 1,\dots,N,
\end{equation}
a genuine convex constraint: with $\sum_i w_i=1$ and no weight above $1/K$, at least $K$ names are
mathematically guaranteed positive weight. Every number from here uses $K=5$ ($w_i\le20\%$);
realized counts then range $5$--$20$, mean $12.3$ (A) and $5$--$15$, mean $10.4$ (B).
\Cref{tab:beforeafter} compares A/B with and without the cap; C, D, E, F are untouched.

The cap does not merely trade return for lower risk --- return \emph{rises} for both A and B while
volatility and drawdown fall by roughly a third, because it does not discourage the concentrated
bets that hurt performance, it makes them infeasible. From here, ``A'' and ``B'' refer to the
capped variant unless stated otherwise.

\subsection{A cautionary extension: long-short without a leverage constraint}\label{sec:ef}

E and F keep \cref{eq:main} exactly as stated --- short positions allowed, no bound on
$\norm{w}_1$ beyond the soft $\lambda$ penalty --- and both are wiped out (\cref{fig:equity6}).
Gross exposure is unbounded whenever the grid selects a small or zero $\lambda$, which it did
repeatedly: a cheaply-leveraged combination can look almost risk-free against a shrinkage-smoothed
but still noisy $\hat\Sigma$ without being risk-free out of sample. On F's worst day (2025-06-02),
no individual ticker moved more than $\pm7\%$, yet a portfolio of $49$ positions above $100\%$ of
capital each (gross exposure $133\times$) lost $39\%$ in that one session --- a few such sessions
inside an un-rebalanced month are enough to breach $-100\%$, at which point the simulator floors
the loss and marks the remainder of the month at $0\%$. The pattern recurred for both E and F
across independent periods, a structural consequence of selecting $(\lambda,\gamma)$, and for F
$\kappa$ too, by validation Sharpe with no cap on $\norm{w}_1$ anywhere. The natural fix, a hard
leverage cap $\norm{w}_1\le L$, is left for future work (\cref{sec:conclusion}); the point here is
the negative result: a per-position weight cap, effective for A/B, would \emph{not} address this
failure, because the pathology is \emph{total} exposure, not concentration in one name.

\subsection{Interpretation}\label{sec:interpretation}

\textbf{The robust term does not earn its keep, out of sample either.} A and B differ only in
whether $\kappa$ is searched or fixed at $0$; B's Sharpe ($1.39$) exceeds A's ($1.19$), and A's own
search picks $\kappa=0$ in $18$ of $25$ periods --- corroborating \cref{sec:insample}: because
$\kappa\norm{\Ssqrt w}_2$ pulls with constant magnitude regardless of scale, it behaves like a
competing objective rather than a caution dial, and that trade consistently loses here.

\textbf{Both long-only variants beat both passive benchmarks}, so the naive-diversification result
of \citet{demiguel2009} does not hold here --- but not for free: B's and A's fees
($2.15\%$/$3.00\%$, from $42.9\%$/$60.1\%$ monthly turnover) run $4$--$6\times$ C's ($0.49\%$),
driven by $\gamma$ swinging across $\{1,5,10\}$ between adjacent months without settling
(\cref{app:backtest}) --- a symptom of a noisy six-month validation window, not of the cap.

\textbf{Cost is kept external to \cref{eq:main}} rather than added as a turnover penalty
$\eta\norm{w-w_{\text{prev}}}_1$; three reasons are given in \cref{app:turnover-design}, briefly:
$w_{\text{prev}}$ is meaningful only in this multi-period setting, not the single-period model of
\cref{sec:modeling}; $\eta$ would be a fourth hyperparameter fit to the same unstable six-month
window; and keeping cost external keeps \cref{tab:beforeafter}'s improvement attributable cleanly
to the concentration cap rather than a simultaneously-tuned cost term.

\textbf{The long-short extension is the sharpest lesson.} E and F are not merely worse than A--D,
they are categorically different: nothing in their formulation bounds total leverage the way the
long-only constraint set implicitly does. This is not a critique of allowing $\lambda$ or short
positions in \cref{eq:main} --- it is evidence that validation-Sharpe selection with no leverage
bound anywhere will occasionally pick an unbounded one, and unbounded leverage combined with
ordinary daily volatility is catastrophic regardless of how well-motivated the convex program is.
