\section{Walk-Forward Out-of-Sample Backtest}\label{sec:backtest}

\subsection{Why the in-sample results are not enough}

\Cref{sec:insample} estimates $\muhat$ and $\Sig$ from the full return panel and then optimizes
against those same estimates, so $w^\star$ is optimal for data it has already seen. A portfolio
can look excellent under that procedure purely because $\muhat$ absorbed the noise of that
particular period, and nothing in the in-sample objective distinguishes that case from genuine
predictive content. Separating them requires an evaluation in which \textbf{every decision at
time $t$ uses only data dated strictly before $t$}.

\subsection{Design}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Rolling window.} Each rebalance uses the 24 months immediately preceding it,
        split into 18 months for estimation ($E$) and the following 6 for validation ($V$); the
        whole window slides forward by exactly 1 month each period (\cref{app:timeline} works a
        month-numbered example over three periods).
  \item \textbf{Schedule.} Monthly, on the first trading day of each month.
  \item \textbf{Long-only.} The deployed problem adds $w \ge 0$, unlike \cref{eq:main}, since
        the target investor has no short access. As shown in \cref{sec:method}, the simplex
        constraint makes $\norm{w}_1 \equiv 1$, so $\lambda$ cannot affect the solution and is
        dropped from the search rather than tuned pointlessly.
  \item \textbf{Hyperparameter selection, per period.} The grid
        $(\kappa,\gamma) \in \{0, 0.5, 1, 2\} \times \{1, 5, 10\}$ --- twelve combinations --- is
        fitted on $E$ and scored by Sharpe ratio ($r_f = 0$) on $V$, which was not used for
        fitting; the winner is refitted on $E \cup V$ to give the weights actually deployed.
  \item \textbf{Shrinkage.} Ledoit--Wolf \citep{ledoit2004} throughout, for both selection and
        the deployed fit.
  \item \textbf{Costs.} $0.20\%$ of turnover per rebalance, where turnover sums the
        \emph{absolute} weight change across all 98 tickers relative to the drifted portfolio
        just before rebalancing, not last period's target (\cref{app:timeline} works a numeric
        example); the first period carries turnover $1$, the portfolio being bought from cash.
  \item \textbf{Benchmark.} Equal-weight $1/N$ on the identical schedule under the identical fee
        formula, so the comparison is like-for-like rather than a fully-costed strategy against a
        frictionless index.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Interpretation}

\textbf{The win is real.} The strategy beats equal-weight $1/N$ on both cumulative return and
Sharpe ratio --- not a foregone conclusion, since $1/N$ is a notoriously difficult baseline and
the finding that optimized portfolios routinely fail to beat it out of sample is well established
\citep{demiguel2009}. A loss would have been reported just as readily: the backtest was built
before its outcome was known.

\textbf{The win is narrow, and it is paid for.} Volatility is roughly $44\%$ higher than the
benchmark's ($28.68\%$ against $19.88\%$) and the maximum drawdown about $1.8$ times deeper
($-37.89\%$ against $-20.91\%$). The Sharpe ratio already nets this out, so the risk-adjusted
edge ($0.5244$ against $0.4523$) is genuine --- but it is modest, and an investor experiences the
drawdown directly rather than experiencing the Sharpe ratio.

\textbf{Costs are the binding practical problem.} Total fees are about seven times the
benchmark's ($3.44\%$ against $0.49\%$) over the same 25 rebalances, driven by average monthly
turnover near $69\%$. The cause is diagnosable: because $(\kappa,\gamma)$ is re-selected every
period against a short and therefore noisy six-month validation window, $\gamma$ swings across
$\{1, 5, 10\}$ between consecutive months without settling, and each swing rewrites the target
portfolio wholesale. A secondary observation from the same record is that $\kappa$ is selected as
$0$ in most periods: a short-horizon Sharpe criterion rarely rewards the robust term on this
data, consistent with robustness protecting against tail scenarios a two-year sample may not
contain. Against a raw cumulative return advantage of roughly nine percentage points, three
points of fees --- before slippage or market impact --- consumes a material share of the gain.
