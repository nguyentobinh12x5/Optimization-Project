\section{Walk-Forward Out-of-Sample Backtest}\label{sec:backtest}

\subsection{Why the in-sample results are not enough}

\Cref{sec:insample} estimates $\muhat$ and $\Sig$ from the full return panel and then optimizes
against those same estimates, so $w^\star$ is optimal for data it has already seen. A portfolio
can look excellent purely because $\muhat$ absorbed the noise of that period, and nothing in the
in-sample objective distinguishes that from genuine predictive content. Separating them requires
an evaluation in which \textbf{every decision at time $t$ uses only data dated strictly before
$t$}.

\subsection{Design}

Each rebalance --- monthly, on the first trading day, 494 out-of-sample days and 25 rebalances in
total --- uses the trailing 24 months, split into 18 for estimation ($E$) and 6 for validation
($V$), sliding forward one month at a time, as \cref{tab:timeline} makes concrete
(\cref{app:timeline} works a full turnover and transaction-cost example).

Within each period, four steps run in order: (1) fit each of the 12 $(\kappa,\gamma)$
candidates on $E$; (2) score each by the Sharpe ratio it realizes on $V$, data the fit never
saw; (3) keep the winning $(\kappa^\star,\gamma^\star)$; (4) re-estimate $\muhat,\Sig$ on the
full $E \cup V$ (all 24 months) and refit at $(\kappa^\star,\gamma^\star)$ --- these are the
weights actually deployed for the single next month. Because the window advances by only 1
month while $E \cup V$ spans 24, \textbf{consecutive periods share 23 of 24 months of data}
(period 2's window is period 1's window shifted by one month) --- an overlap worth stating
precisely because the Interpretation below (\cref{sec:interpretation}) nonetheless finds
$(\kappa,\gamma)$ swinging between adjacent periods: the instability is not from the windows
being unrelated, but from a 6-month validation slice being a noisy yardstick even when the
underlying data barely changes from one period to the next.

\textbf{Six variants} are compared, \textbf{long-only} against \textbf{long-short}: a long-only
portfolio forbids short positions ($w\ge0$, every ticker held or not held at all), while a
long-short portfolio allows $w_i<0$, a bet that ticker $i$ falls; and, independently, $\kappa$ free
against fixed at $0$: \textbf{A} (Robust, long-only, $(\kappa,\gamma)\in\{0,0.5,1,2\}\times
\{1,5,10\}$), \textbf{B} (Classical, long-only, $\kappa=0$, $\gamma\in\{1,5,10\}$), \textbf{E}
(Sparse-only, long-short, $\kappa=0$, $(\lambda,\gamma)\in\{0,0.001,0.005,0.01,0.02\}\times
\{1,5,10\}$), and \textbf{F} (Full equation, long-short, all three free, 60 combinations). A and B
add $w\ge0$, absent from \cref{eq:main}; the simplex constraint then makes $\norm{w}_1\equiv1$
(\cref{eq:leverage}), so $\lambda$ is dropped from their search rather than tuned pointlessly ---
neither, therefore, has an active sparsity term, and any concentration they show is a free
consequence of the simplex geometry, not a designed effect (\cref{sec:results7}). E fixes $\kappa=0$
and searches $\lambda$, so its sparsity \emph{is} designed. F searches all three at once. Two
passive benchmarks, both built from the identical 98-ticker universe rather than an external index
such as VN30 (a different 30-ticker basket): \textbf{C} rebalances to equal weight $1/N$ on the
same schedule and fees as A/B; \textbf{D} buys equal weight once and holds, untouched, to the end.

For every variant, its grid is fitted on $E$ and scored by Sharpe ratio ($r_f=0$) on $V$; the
winner is refitted on $E\cup V$ for the deployed weights. Sharpe, not raw return, is used
uniformly so the comparison is not an artifact of differing selection criteria. Shrinkage is
Ledoit--Wolf \citep{ledoit2004} throughout. Costs are $0.20\%$ of turnover per rebalance, turnover
summing \emph{absolute} weight change against the drifted (not the previous target) portfolio
(\cref{app:timeline}); the first period carries turnover $1$. All six variants are solved with
CVXPY/CLARABEL \citep{diamond2016, clarabel2024}, the same solver used throughout
\cref{sec:insample} --- there is one solver in this project, applied consistently from the
in-sample results to every walk-forward rebalance, rather than a different implementation per
section. Each solve completes in roughly a dozen interior-point iterations
(\cref{sec:insample}), so a grid search re-solving the problem 12--60 times per rebalance across
25 rebalances still runs in well under a minute.

{\footnotesize

\]

{\footnotesize

\subsection{A concentration problem, and a constraint to fix it}\label{sec:cap}

A and B already beat both benchmarks in \cref{tab:methods}. But the \emph{number of names held}
swings wildly from month to month --- B's trace (\cref{tab:bmonthly}) ranges from $1$ to $14$,
mean $7.56$, and in February 2025 the entire portfolio concentrated in a single ticker on the
strength of its trailing 24-month sample mean and lost $9.47\%$ the following month when that edge
did not persist. Nothing in \cref{eq:main} stops this: a long-only quadratic program's KKT
conditions place the optimum at a vertex or a low-dimensional face of the simplex, with no floor
on how many names that face touches.

\textbf{Adding a constraint an investor would actually want.} A real investor generally wants a
portfolio that always holds at least a handful of names, both to limit the damage from any single
estimate being wrong and because a one-ticker "portfolio" is not diversification by any reasonable
definition. Rather than hard-code a single cap, we parameterize it by $K$, the minimum number of
names the investor wants held at once --- a choice the investor makes, not the model --- and add
to \cref{eq:main}, for A and B only,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:capk}
w_i \le \frac{1}{K}, \qquad i = 1,\dots,N,
\end{equation}
applied identically during selection and deployment (the underlying solver,
\texttt{cvxpy\_solve\_long\_only} in \texttt{src/cvxpy\_check.py}, already exposes this as a free
\texttt{max\_weight} argument equal to $1/K$, rather than a number hard-coded into the objective).
This is a genuine convex constraint, not a heuristic --- with $\sum_i w_i=1$ and no weight above
$1/K$, at least $K$ names are mathematically guaranteed to hold positive weight (not, however, an
upper bound on how many are held). $K$ is a dial an investor turns to taste: $K=5$ gives a $20\%$
per-name cap, $K=10$ tightens it to $10\%$, and so on, trading a lower floor on diversification
against more freedom for the optimizer to concentrate in its best-ranked names. Every number
reported from here on uses $K=5$ ($w_i\le20\%$), the setting used throughout this report; once
added, realized counts range $5$--$20$, mean $12.3$, for A and $5$--$15$, mean $10.4$, for B.
\Cref{tab:beforeafter} compares A and B with and without it; C, D, E, F are untouched by this
constraint and keep the values already reported in \cref{tab:methods}.

The cap does not merely trade return for lower risk, the usual effect of a constraint --- here
return \emph{rises} for both A and B while volatility and drawdown both fall by roughly a third,
because the constraint does not discourage the concentrated bets that hurt performance, it makes
them infeasible. From here on, "A" and "B" refer to the capped variant unless stated otherwise.

\subsection{A cautionary extension: long-short without a leverage constraint}\label{sec:ef}

E and F keep \cref{eq:main} exactly as stated --- short positions allowed, no bound on
$\norm{w}_1$ beyond the soft $\lambda$ penalty --- and both are wiped out
(\cref{fig:equity6,fig:drawdown6}). Gross exposure is unbounded whenever the grid selects a small or zero
$\lambda$, which it did repeatedly: a cheaply-leveraged combination can look almost risk-free
against a shrinkage-smoothed but still noisy $\hat\Sigma$ without being risk-free out of sample.
On F's worst day (2025-06-02), no individual ticker moved more than $\pm7\%$, yet a portfolio of
$49$ positions above $100\%$ of capital each (gross exposure $133\times$) lost $39\%$ in that one
session --- a few such sessions inside an un-rebalanced month are enough to breach $-100\%$, at
which point \texttt{\_simulate\_period} floors the loss and marks the remainder of the month at
$0\%$. The pattern recurred for both E and F across independent periods: a structural consequence
of selecting $(\lambda,\gamma)$, and for F $\kappa$ too, by validation Sharpe with no cap on
$\norm{w}_1$ anywhere. The natural fix, a hard leverage cap $\norm{w}_1\le L$, is a different
constraint shape from A/B's per-ticker cap and is left for future work (\cref{sec:conclusion})
rather than a same-day patch --- the point of this section is the negative result: a per-position
weight cap, effective for A/B, would \emph{not} address this failure, because the pathology is
\emph{total} exposure, not concentration in one name.

\subsection{Interpretation}\label{sec:interpretation}

\textbf{The robust term does not earn its keep, out of sample as well as in-sample.} A and B
differ only in whether $\kappa$ is searched or fixed at $0$, under an identical selection
criterion and concentration cap. B's Sharpe ($1.39$) exceeds A's ($1.19$), and A's own search picks
$\kappa=0$ in $18$ of $25$ periods. This corroborates \cref{sec:insample} out of sample: because
$\kappa\norm{\Ssqrt w}_2$ pulls with constant magnitude regardless of scale, it behaves like a
second, competing objective rather than a caution dial, and on this universe that trade
consistently loses.

\textbf{Both long-only variants beat both passive benchmarks}, so the naive-diversification result
of \citet{demiguel2009} does not hold here, though not for free: B's volatility and drawdown still
exceed both benchmarks', and its fees ($2.15\%$, turnover $42.9\%$/month) and A's ($3.00\%$,
$60.1\%$) run $4$--$6\times$ C's ($0.49\%$) and dwarf D's single purchase. \Cref{fig:selectedparams}
shows the cause: $\gamma$ still swings across $\{1,5,10\}$ between adjacent months without
settling --- the cap bounds how concentrated a period's portfolio can be, not how much it changes
from the last one, the same short-validation-window instability flagged in \cref{sec:conclusion}.

\textbf{Should this cost be inside the optimization instead of outside it?} Every number above
treats transaction cost as a friction applied \emph{after} $w^\star$ is chosen ---
\cref{eq:main} has no term that sees $w_{\text{prev}}$ at all, so the optimizer that picks
$\gamma=1$ this month has no way to know that doing so will force a $60\%$ turnover against last
month's holdings. The natural alternative is to add a turnover penalty
$\eta\norm{w-w_{\text{prev}}}_1$ directly to \cref{eq:main} at each rebalance, letting the
optimizer trade expected return against \emph{both} risk and rebalancing cost simultaneously,
which is the standard treatment in the transaction-cost portfolio literature. Three reasons this
report keeps cost as a post-hoc evaluation deduction instead: first, \cref{eq:main} is stated and
solved once in \cref{sec:insample} as a single-period problem, and $w_{\text{prev}}$ is
meaningful only in the multi-period walk-forward setting of this section --- folding it in would
mean the core model of \cref{sec:modeling} is no longer the same model actually deployed, which
this report's structure deliberately avoids. Second, $\eta$ would be a fourth hyperparameter
selected on the same six-month validation window already shown (\cref{sec:results7},
\cref{fig:selectedparams}) to select $\gamma$ unstably --- adding a cost-aware term without first
fixing that instability risks fitting $\eta$ to six months of noise rather than to a genuine
cost--return trade-off. Third, and most concretely, the concentration cap of \cref{sec:cap} was
added \emph{because} of a diversification concern (\cref{tab:bmonthly}), not a cost concern, yet
it happens to cut A's and B's turnover as a side effect --- keeping cost external to
\cref{eq:main} keeps that attribution clean: \cref{tab:beforeafter}'s improvement is
demonstrably from the cap, not confounded with a simultaneously-tuned cost penalty. A cost-aware
variant, $\eta\norm{w-w_{\text{prev}}}_1$ added once the $\gamma$-selection instability above is
addressed, is accordingly left as the natural next extension rather than folded in here.

\textbf{The long-short extension is the sharpest lesson here.} E and F are not merely worse than
A--D, they are categorically different, because nothing in their formulation bounds total leverage
the way the long-only constraint set implicitly does. This is not a critique of allowing $\lambda$
or short positions in principle --- \cref{eq:main} still permits both, deliberately --- it is
evidence that a search selecting parameters by short-window validation performance, with no
leverage constraint anywhere in the objective, will occasionally select an unbounded one, and
unbounded leverage combined with ordinary daily volatility is catastrophic regardless of how
well-motivated the underlying convex program is.
