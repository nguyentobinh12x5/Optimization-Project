\section{Discussion, Limitations, and Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

\subsection{Computational efficiency and solution quality}

The two solvers trade off as expected (\cref{sec:cost}): CLARABEL wins $\sim6\times$ on speed at
$N=98$ ($25$\,ms/$14$ $O(N^3)$ iterations vs.\ $146$\,ms/$528$ $O(N^2)$ iterations), an ordering
that would reverse at $N$ in the thousands and that makes the walk-forward's $12$--$60$ re-solves
per rebalance practical. On solution quality, convexity guarantees any correct solver reaches the
global optimum, and \cref{tab:verification} confirms two algorithms sharing no code agree to
$10^{-8}$--$10^{-6}$ relative terms with identical active sets in every configuration but one,
reported rather than omitted (\cref{sec:verification}); the proximal method's exact zeros are
what justify the active-set thresholds used throughout.

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Survivorship bias:} current VN100 tickers applied backward over four years;
        delisted or removed tickers are absent.
  \item \textbf{$\muhat$ remains a poor estimator:} an early unconstrained run put a month's
        capital in one ticker on its trailing sample mean, then lost $9.47\%$ the next month. The
        robust term does not blunt this --- $\kappa$ is not selected as helpful, and this now
        holds out of sample too ($\kappa=0$ selected in $18$ of $25$ periods) --- the
        concentration cap does, by making such bets infeasible rather than merely discouraged.
  \item \textbf{No prediction model:} $\muhat$ is always the trailing sample mean, never a
        forecast. A predict-then-optimize benchmark (predictor $\to$ same \cref{eq:main}) is a
        genuinely separate follow-on with new look-ahead risk, not attempted here.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Conclusion}

\textbf{This paper does not argue that one method is best.} As stated from the outset
(\cref{sec:identification}), no single formula is best for every market. The contribution is
instead a single convex objective, \cref{eq:main}, whose terms can be switched on, off, or tuned
to collapse into \textbf{four distinct investment strategies} as special cases --- robust or
classical, long-only or long-short --- plus two passive benchmarks used for comparison. Which of
the four an investor should actually run is an empirical question that depends on their risk
tolerance and holding preferences and on the statistical character of the specific market traded;
the results below report what that choice looked like for VN100 over this sample, not a universal
ranking.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Formulation:} a convex, second-order-cone representable program in which the
        robust term is derived as an exact worst-case objective over an ellipsoidal uncertainty
        set, and the $\ell_1$ penalty serves both as a sparsity surrogate and a leverage cap.

  \item \textbf{Solver:} a proximal-subgradient method (\cref{sec:algorithm}) whose prox step
        handles the $\ell_1$ penalty and budget constraint jointly and exactly, by bisection, so
        soft-thresholded zeros survive rather than being revived by a separate projection;
        convexity makes that local iteration globally optimal, and an independent interior-point
        solve confirms it to six significant figures with identical active sets
        (\cref{sec:verification}).

  \item \textbf{Out-of-sample result:} six variants were walk-forward tested over $494$ trading
        days and $25$ monthly rebalances (\cref{sec:backtest}). The two long-only variants beat
        both passive benchmarks on a risk-adjusted basis, with the classical ($\kappa=0$) variant
        outperforming its robust counterpart --- confirming out of sample that the robust term does
        not help on this universe --- at $4$--$6\times$ the benchmarks' fees from monthly
        re-selection of the risk-aversion parameter.

  \item \textbf{What drove the long-only result:} a per-ticker concentration constraint, added
        after an early run's single-ticker portfolio lost $9.47\%$ in a month, accounts for most
        of that improvement and is a substantive finding, not a tuning detail.

  \item \textbf{What broke the long-short variants:} the unconstrained long-short variants, by
        contrast, suffered total capital loss --- categorically worse than anything in the
        long-only comparison, traced to unbounded leverage interacting with short-window parameter
        selection.
\end{itemize}

Taken together, the long-only, concentration-capped configuration is the one this sample
recommends for an investor who wants a bounded, self-financing position on VN100 today; the
robust and long-short terms remain available in \cref{eq:main} and may earn their keep on a
market with noisier means, higher correlation risk, or a bound on total leverage --- a question
left open, not settled, by this study.
