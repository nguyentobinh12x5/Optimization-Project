\section{Discussion, Limitations, and Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

\subsection{Computational efficiency and solution quality}

CLARABEL solves \cref{eq:main} in roughly a dozen interior-point iterations at $N = 98$
(\cref{tab:demo}: 14 and 11 for the two representative solves), each forming and factorizing a
KKT system at $O(N^3)$, in tens of milliseconds. That cost is what makes the walk-forward
backtest of \cref{sec:backtest} practical: 25 rebalances, each re-solving the problem 12--60
times across a parameter grid, still complete in well under a minute. The tradeoff is the one an
interior-point method always makes at larger scale --- the $O(N^3)$ factorization cost would
dominate at $N$ in the thousands, where a first-order method's cheaper $O(N^2)$ iterations become
attractive despite needing more of them --- but at this problem's size that scale is not reached,
so the choice is not close. On solution quality: the budget constraint is satisfied to $10^{-12}$,
and convexity (\cref{sec:correctness}) guarantees the reported optimum is global, not merely a
point some local search happened to stop at.

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Survivorship bias:} current VN100 membership applied backward over four years,
        so delisted and removed tickers are absent.
  \item \textbf{$\muhat$ remains a poor estimator:} \cref{sec:backtest} shows the consequence
        concretely --- an early, unconstrained long-only run put a full month's capital in one
        ticker on the strength of its trailing sample mean, then lost $9.47\%$ the next month. The
        robust term does not blunt this ($\kappa$ is not selected as helpful at all); a per-ticker
        weight cap does, by making such bets infeasible rather than merely discouraged.
  \item \textbf{No prediction model, and no predict-then-optimize benchmark:} every $\muhat$ in
        this report is the trailing sample mean over the estimation window --- a backward-looking
        statistic, not a forecast, and \cref{sec:identification} states this as a modelling
        choice rather than treating it as settled. The natural benchmark this report does
        \emph{not} run is a predict-then-optimize pipeline: fit a return predictor (even a simple
        one, e.g.\ ridge regression or an EWMA-momentum score, on the same estimation window)
        in place of the sample mean, feed its output into the identical \cref{eq:main}, and
        walk-forward test it against A--F exactly as designed. Two things make this a genuinely
        separate follow-on rather than a small addition: it introduces a new source of look-ahead
        risk distinct from the ones already guarded against here (a predictor must be refit,
        not just re-estimated, inside each rolling window), and it changes the object under
        test from ``which configuration of one convex objective wins'' to ``does a better
        $\muhat$ matter more than which configuration is chosen'' --- a different empirical
        question, worth asking, but not the one this report answers.
  \item \textbf{The $\kappa$ finding now holds out of sample, too:} B outperforms A on Sharpe
        under an identical selection criterion, and $\kappa=0$ is selected in $18$ of $25$
        periods --- still an observation on one covariance structure, but not an in-sample
        artifact.
  \item \textbf{Unconstrained leverage is a distinct, more severe failure mode:} \cref{sec:ef}'s
        long-short variants, with no cap on gross exposure $\norm{w}_1$, suffered total capital
        loss --- not merely higher risk than the long-only variants, but a different failure kind,
        driven by validation-Sharpe selection occasionally choosing a near-zero $\lambda$ against a
        still-noisy $\hat\Sigma$. A leverage cap $\norm{w}_1\le L$ is the natural fix and is left
        as future work.
  \item \textbf{The out-of-sample window is short:} roughly 24 months, a single market regime, not
        extrapolable to other cycles.
  \item \textbf{Validation overfitting:} selecting hyperparameters on a six-month window mildly
        fits the validation set --- inherent to short-window walk-forward, and the direct cause
        both of A/B's turnover-driven cost disadvantage and, more severely, of \cref{sec:ef}'s
        leverage episodes.
  \item \textbf{A single historical path:} no bootstrap, so no confidence interval accompanies the
        reported Sharpe ratios or drawdowns.
  \item \textbf{Costs are modelled crudely:} flat $0.20\%$ of turnover, no market-impact, slippage,
        or margin-call mechanism --- the last mattering specifically for \cref{sec:ef}, where a
        real broker would force deleveraging long before a $100\times$-exposure position reached
        total loss.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Conclusion}

The problem was formulated as a convex, second-order-cone representable program in which the
robust term is derived as an exact worst-case objective over an ellipsoidal uncertainty set
rather than assumed, and the $\ell_1$ penalty serves both as a convex surrogate for the number of
holdings and as a cap on gross leverage. Convexity and the conic reformulation \cref{eq:socp}
together license solving it directly with CVXPY/CLARABEL: every local minimum is global, so a
general-purpose interior-point solver's reported optimum is provably the optimum, with no
custom algorithm and no independent check required to trust it. Out of sample, six variants
were walk-forward tested over 494 trading days
and 25 monthly rebalances (\cref{sec:backtest}). The two long-only variants beat both passive
benchmarks on a risk-adjusted basis, the classical ($\kappa=0$) variant outperforming its robust
counterpart --- confirming out of sample that the robust term does not help on this universe ---
at $4$--$6\times$ the benchmarks' fees from monthly re-selection of the risk-aversion parameter. A
per-ticker concentration constraint, added after an early run's single-ticker portfolio lost
$9.47\%$ in a month, accounts for most of that improvement and is a substantive finding in its own
right, not a tuning detail. The unconstrained long-short variants, by contrast, suffered total
capital loss --- categorically worse than anything in the long-only comparison, traced to
unbounded leverage interacting with short-window parameter selection.

The methodological lesson is a narrower one than a hand-written implementation would have taught:
at $N = 98$, a convex, conic-representable objective is exactly the class of problem an
off-the-shelf interior-point solver is built for, and convexity's global-optimality guarantee
removes the need to verify its answer against anything else. The scale where a custom first-order
method would earn its added complexity --- $N$ in the thousands, or a nonconvex extension no
conic solver can represent --- is not this one.\label{sec:endofbody}
