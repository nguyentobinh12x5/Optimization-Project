\section{Discussion, Limitations, and Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

\subsection{Computational efficiency and solution quality}

The two solvers trade off as expected (\cref{sec:cost}): CLARABEL wins $\sim6\times$ on speed at
$N=98$ ($25$\,ms/$14$ $O(N^3)$ iterations vs.\ $146$\,ms/$528$ $O(N^2)$ iterations), an ordering
that would reverse at $N$ in the thousands and that makes the walk-forward's $12$--$60$ re-solves
per rebalance practical. On solution quality, convexity guarantees any correct solver reaches the
global optimum, and \cref{tab:verification} confirms two algorithms sharing no code agree to
$10^{-8}$--$10^{-6}$ relative terms with identical active sets in every configuration but one,
reported rather than omitted (\cref{sec:verification}); the proximal method's exact zeros are
what justify the active-set thresholds used throughout.

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Survivorship bias:} current VN100 membership applied backward over four years;
        delisted or removed tickers are absent.
  \item \textbf{$\muhat$ remains a poor estimator:} an early unconstrained run put a month's
        capital in one ticker on its trailing sample mean, then lost $9.47\%$ the next month. The
        robust term does not blunt this --- $\kappa$ is not selected as helpful, and this now
        holds out of sample too ($\kappa=0$ selected in $18$ of $25$ periods) --- the
        concentration cap does, by making such bets infeasible rather than merely discouraged.
  \item \textbf{No prediction model:} $\muhat$ is always the trailing sample mean, never a
        forecast. A predict-then-optimize benchmark (predictor $\to$ same \cref{eq:main}) is a
        genuinely separate follow-on with new look-ahead risk, not attempted here.
  \item \textbf{Unconstrained leverage is a distinct, more severe failure mode:} E/F's total
        capital loss is driven by validation-Sharpe selection choosing near-zero $\lambda$ against
        a still-noisy $\hat\Sigma$, and structurally so, since arithmetic Sharpe is blind to
        volatility drag (\cref{sec:results7}). A leverage cap $\norm{w}_1\le L$ is the natural
        fix, left as future work.
  \item \textbf{Other caveats:} a short, single-regime OOS window ($\sim24$ months); a six-month
        validation slice that mildly overfits hyperparameters (the direct cause of A/B's turnover
        cost and E/F's leverage episodes); a single historical path, so no bootstrap confidence
        interval; and a flat $0.20\%$-of-turnover cost model with no slippage, market-impact, or
        margin calls --- the last mattering for \cref{sec:ef}, where a real broker would force
        deleveraging long before a $100\times$-exposure position reached total loss.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Conclusion}

The problem was formulated as a convex, second-order-cone representable program in which the
robust term is derived as an exact worst-case objective over an ellipsoidal uncertainty set, and
the $\ell_1$ penalty serves both as a sparsity surrogate and a leverage cap. It was solved by a
proximal-subgradient method (\cref{sec:algorithm}) whose prox step handles the $\ell_1$ penalty
and budget constraint jointly and exactly, by bisection, so soft-thresholded zeros survive rather
than being revived by a separate projection; convexity makes that local iteration globally
optimal, and an independent interior-point solve confirms it to six significant figures with
identical active sets (\cref{sec:verification}). Out of sample, six variants were walk-forward
tested over $494$ trading days and $25$ monthly rebalances (\cref{sec:backtest}): the two
long-only variants beat both passive benchmarks on a risk-adjusted basis, the classical
($\kappa=0$) variant outperforming its robust counterpart --- confirming out of sample that the
robust term does not help on this universe --- at $4$--$6\times$ the benchmarks' fees from monthly
re-selection of the risk-aversion parameter. A per-ticker concentration constraint, added after an
early run's single-ticker portfolio lost $9.47\%$ in a month, accounts for most of that
improvement and is a substantive finding, not a tuning detail. The unconstrained long-short
variants, by contrast, suffered total capital loss --- categorically worse than anything in the
long-only comparison, traced to unbounded leverage interacting with short-window parameter
selection.

The methodological lesson concerns what convexity does and does not buy. It is a property of the
\emph{problem}: it promotes the limit of a local, first-order iteration into the global optimum,
and it guarantees that two correct solvers must agree. It says nothing about whether a given
implementation is correct, and a mis-derived subgradient or a prematurely terminated root-find
returns a feasible-looking answer in silence. Writing the algorithm and checking it against an
independent solver are therefore complementary rather than redundant --- the first is what makes
the sparsity counts exact and the method extensible beyond what a conic solver can represent, the
second is what makes either believable.\label{sec:endofbody}
